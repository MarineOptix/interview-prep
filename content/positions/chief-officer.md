## Q: How do you plan and supervise cargo operations?

Planning starts before arrival. I check the voyage orders and the cargo details, prepare the loading or discharging plan on the loading computer, and confirm that stability, shear forces, bending moments and drafts stay within limits at every stage, not only at the end.

Then I agree the plan with the terminal: rates, sequence, ballast operations, stops and communication. The plan goes to the duty officers with clear standing orders: what to check every hour, what limits to watch and when to call me.

During operations I am on deck at the critical stages: start, topping off, change of grades or holds, and completion. I compare the actual figures with the plan, check the moorings and draft, and keep the Master informed. After completion I check the final figures and documents before signing anything.

The main lesson I have learned is that the plan is only as good as the communication behind it. Most cargo incidents start with a misunderstanding between ship and shore.

## Q: What is the free surface effect and how do you control it?

When a tank is partly filled, the liquid moves as the ship heels and shifts its centre of gravity towards the low side. The effect is a virtual rise of the ship's centre of gravity and a reduction of the effective metacentric height (GM). Several slack tanks at the same time can reduce GM dangerously, even if each one looks harmless on its own.

To control it, I:

- Plan ballast and fuel operations so that as few tanks as possible are slack at the same time.
- Press up or empty tanks rather than leaving them half full, especially wide tanks.
- Always include free surface corrections in the stability calculation, using the loading computer and the stability booklet.
- Watch for signs at sea: a slow, heavy roll or an unexplained list may mean low GM, not a cargo shift.

On tankers it is especially important during simultaneous cargo and ballast operations, when many tanks can be slack at once.

## Q: You are the responsible officer for an enclosed space entry. What is your procedure?

I follow the company's permit-to-work procedure, and I do not allow entry until every condition is met:

1. **Risk assessment** and a toolbox talk with everyone involved, including the rescue plan.
2. **Isolation**: pipelines, pumps and inert gas isolated and tagged; the space ventilated well in advance and ventilation kept running.
3. **Atmosphere testing** with calibrated instruments at different levels: oxygen 21% by volume, hydrocarbons below 1% of the lower flammable limit, toxic gases below their exposure limits.
4. **Standby person** at the entrance with communication, breathing apparatus and rescue equipment ready.
5. **Permit** signed by the Master or the authorised officer, with a time limit, and the bridge informed.

During the entry the atmosphere is monitored continuously, and anyone may stop the job. The most important rule I give the team is that no one enters to rescue a colleague without breathing apparatus: many enclosed space deaths are would-be rescuers.

## Q: How does your ship comply with the Ballast Water Management Convention?

The ship has an approved Ballast Water Management Plan and a Ballast Water Record Book, and most ships now meet the D-2 performance standard with an approved ballast water treatment system. As Chief Officer I am usually the responsible officer for ballast operations.

In practice this means:

- Operating the treatment system according to the manufacturer's manual, including filter cleaning and UV or chemical treatment checks.
- Recording every ballast uptake, treatment, transfer and discharge in the record book, with positions, quantities and times.
- Checking the port's local requirements in advance: some ports and countries have additional rules or require reports before arrival.
- Reporting any system failure to the company immediately and agreeing a contingency measure before discharging ballast.

Port State inspectors check the record book against the ship's position and cargo operations, so accuracy and consistency are essential.

## Q: How do you manage the deck crew and plan deck maintenance?

I plan maintenance with the planned maintenance system and the ship's trading pattern: weather, port stays and inspections. Each week I agree priorities with the Bosun, and each morning he organises the work with the crew and reports back at the end of the day.

Priorities are safety equipment and statutory items first, then anything that affects cargo operations or watertight integrity, such as hatch covers and vents, then preservation and painting.

For the crew, I focus on clear tasks, the right tools and permits, and realistic workloads that respect rest hours. I check the work myself, give feedback, and make sure the Bosun has the authority to run the deck. Good results come from a strong Bosun, so I invest time in that relationship.

I also take training seriously: familiarisation for new joiners, cadet training record books and practical training in mooring and enclosed space rescue.
