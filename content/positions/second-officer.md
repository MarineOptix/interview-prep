## Q: How do you prepare a passage plan?

I follow the four stages of IMO guidelines for voyage planning: appraisal, planning, execution and monitoring.

- **Appraisal**: I collect all the information: charts and ENCs, sailing directions, tide tables, routeing guides, navigational warnings, weather, the ship's draft and condition, and the Master's and company's requirements.
- **Planning**: I draw the berth-to-berth route on ECDIS with safe under-keel clearance, no-go areas, safety contour, wheel-over points, parallel indexing, reporting points, abort points and contingency anchorages. Areas with special rules, such as traffic separation schemes, ECAs and VTS zones, are marked.
- **Execution**: the Master approves the plan and every navigating officer reads and signs it before departure.
- **Monitoring**: during the voyage the officer of the watch checks the ship's position against the plan and updates it for new warnings or weather.

I also run the ECDIS route check and review the alarms it gives, rather than relying on the automatic check alone.

## Q: How do you set the safety contour on ECDIS?

The safety contour separates safe water from unsafe water on the display, and ECDIS uses it for the anti-grounding alarms. I calculate the minimum safe depth as the ship's maximum draft plus the required under-keel clearance from the company's policy, plus allowances for squat and for the height of tide if the company allows it.

If the ENC does not have a contour at exactly that depth, ECDIS selects the next deeper available contour, which may be much deeper than I set. I check which contour is actually displayed, because that changes how the route looks.

I also set the safety depth, which highlights soundings shallower than that value, and the look-ahead area for the alarms. After setting the values, I confirm them with the Master and record them in the passage plan. Setting the values once in port is not enough: they must be updated if the draft changes.

## Q: How do you keep charts and nautical publications up to date?

On an ECDIS ship, I apply the weekly ENC updates from the chart provider and check that all the cells needed for the voyage are installed, licensed and up to date. After each update I review the changes on the route and record the update in the chart correction log.

For temporary and preliminary notices, I check whether they affect our route and plot them on ECDIS as manual updates or notes, because they are not always included in the ENC data.

For publications: Notices to Mariners, the List of Lights, Radio Signals, Sailing Directions and the Tide Tables are updated from the weekly notices, digital or paper, depending on what the ship carries.

Navigational warnings received by NAVTEX and SafetyNET are reviewed every watch and plotted if relevant. Before each voyage I confirm that we have every chart and publication for the route, including for ports we may divert to.

## Q: You receive a DSC distress alert on VHF. What do you do?

First I inform the Master. I do not acknowledge the alert by DSC: under current IMO guidance, a ship normally should not acknowledge a DSC distress alert by DSC, because that can stop the alert from reaching the coast station.

Instead I:

1. Listen on VHF channel 16 for the distress call and message by voice.
2. Note the position, the time and the identity (MMSI) of the ship in distress, and record it in the GMDSS log.
3. If the ship in distress is in our area and no coast station has acknowledged, acknowledge by voice on channel 16 and inform the nearest rescue coordination centre (RCC) by any available means.
4. Follow the RCC's instructions and prepare to assist, under the Master's decision.

If the alert came from our own ship by mistake, I would cancel it immediately by voice on channel 16 and inform the RCC.

## Q: You are on watch in restricted visibility. What do you do?

As soon as visibility drops, I call the Master according to his standing orders, and take the actions required by the COLREGs and the company's procedures:

- **Safe speed** for the visibility and traffic; engines ready for immediate manoeuvre, and the engine room informed.
- **Sound signals** for restricted visibility under Rule 35.
- **Lookout**: an extra lookout posted, and the helmsman on the wheel when needed.
- **Radar**: both radars in use with appropriate ranges, ARPA targets acquired and CPA and TCPA monitored.
- **Navigation lights** switched on.

Under Rule 19, if I detect a vessel by radar only and a close-quarters situation is developing, I take avoiding action in ample time. I avoid altering course to port for a vessel forward of the beam, except when overtaking, and I avoid altering towards a vessel abeam or abaft the beam. If I hear a fog signal forward of the beam, I reduce speed to the minimum needed to keep course and, if necessary, take all way off.
