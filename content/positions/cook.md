## Q: How do you plan menus for a multinational crew?

At the start of the contract I find out who is on board: nationalities, religions, any allergies or medical diets, and what the crew likes. MLC 2006 requires food that is of good quality and quantity and respects the crew's cultural and religious backgrounds.

I plan a weekly menu that rotates, so meals do not repeat too often, with:

- A balanced diet every day: protein, vegetables, fruit, and starch.
- Options for crew who do not eat pork or beef, and vegetarian options when needed; halal meat kept and prepared separately if Muslim crew are on board.
- Dishes from the different nationalities on board, so everyone feels at home sometimes.

I plan within the daily feeding budget agreed with the Master, using provisions efficiently and reducing waste. I also ask for feedback from the crew regularly, because good food is one of the most important things for morale on a long voyage.

## Q: How do you make sure the food you prepare is safe?

I follow food safety principles based on HACCP and the company's procedures:

- **Temperatures**: refrigerated food kept at 0–5 °C, frozen food at −18 °C or below, cooked food reaching at least 75 °C in the core, and hot food held above 63 °C. I check and record fridge and freezer temperatures every day.
- **No cross-contamination**: separate colour-coded boards and knives for raw meat, fish, vegetables and cooked food; raw and cooked food stored separately, with raw meat at the bottom of the fridge.
- **Defrosting** in the fridge, not at room temperature.
- **Personal hygiene**: clean uniform, hand washing, covered cuts, and I report if I am ill.
- **Leftovers** cooled quickly, labelled with the date and used within the company's time limit.

The galley and storerooms are cleaned to a schedule and inspected regularly by the Master or an officer, as MLC requires.

## Q: How do you manage provisions and stores?

I keep a stock list of provisions and check it regularly, so I know what we have and how many days it will last. Before each supply port, I prepare the provision order with the Master based on the menu plan, the number of crew, the voyage length and the budget.

When provisions are delivered I check them: quantity against the order, quality, temperature of chilled and frozen goods, and expiry dates. I reject anything that is damaged or of poor quality.

Storage: everything is labelled with the date, used first in, first out, and stored in the right room: dry store, cold room or freezer. I keep the storerooms clean and secured for sea.

I record consumption and keep the daily cost within the feeding rate. Careful stock control means less waste and no surprises in the middle of a long voyage.

## Q: Oil in a pan catches fire in the galley. What do you do?

1. **Switch off the heat** source immediately, and the galley extraction fan if possible, so the fire does not spread into the ducts.
2. **Smother the fire**: cover the pan with a lid or a fire blanket, or use the wet chemical extinguisher designed for cooking oil fires.
3. **Never use water** on burning oil: it turns to steam instantly and throws burning oil around the galley.
4. **Raise the alarm** and inform the bridge if the fire is not out immediately or if there is any smoke in the ducts.
5. **Leave the pan covered** until it cools down completely, because oil can reignite.

To prevent fires, I never leave hot oil unattended, keep the extraction hoods and filters clean of grease, and make sure the galley fire-fighting equipment is in place and working.

## Q: A crew member complains about the food. How do you handle it?

I listen calmly and take it seriously. A complaint is useful information, and the crew's food is my responsibility.

I ask what exactly the problem is: the taste, the portions, the variety, or a dish that does not fit their diet or religion. If it is a mistake I made, I correct it. If it is a preference, I try to include the dish or the style they like in the menu when possible, and explain honestly if the budget or the provisions do not allow it.

If several people have the same complaint, I discuss it with the Master and change the menu. I also invite feedback regularly, so problems come up early and do not turn into conflict in the mess room.

I never take complaints personally. On a long contract, good communication between the cook and the crew matters as much as the cooking itself.
