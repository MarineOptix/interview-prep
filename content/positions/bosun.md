## Q: How do you organise the daily work of the deck crew?

Every morning I meet the Chief Officer to agree the work for the day, based on the maintenance plan, the weather and the ship's schedule. Then I hold a short toolbox talk with the crew: what we are doing, who does what, the hazards, the PPE and any permits needed, such as working aloft or over the side.

I give each person a job that matches their experience, and I pair new or less experienced crew with someone senior. During the day I move between the jobs, check the quality and safety, and adjust the plan if the weather or operations change.

I keep in mind the rest hours: if the crew worked during mooring at night, the plan for the next day must allow them to rest.

At the end of the day I report to the Chief Officer what was done, what is left, and any defects or needs for stores. Good daily communication with the Chief Officer is the base of a well-run deck.

## Q: What are the main safety rules during mooring operations?

Mooring is one of the most dangerous jobs on deck, so I treat every operation seriously:

- **Toolbox talk** before arrival or departure: the mooring plan, who handles which lines, communication with the bridge.
- **Snap-back zones**: everyone knows them and stays out of them; I never let anyone stand in the bight of a line or between a line and a fairlead.
- **Equipment**: winches and brakes checked, lines and stoppers in good condition, the deck free of oil and obstructions.
- **Communication**: one person in charge at each station, clear radio communication with the bridge, and hand signals agreed with the winch operator.
- **PPE**: helmets, gloves, safety shoes and coveralls with nothing loose.

I keep the number of people at the station to what is needed: people who have no job there are only at risk. If I see an unsafe situation, I stop the operation and inform the bridge.

## Q: What precautions do you take for working aloft or over the side?

Working aloft or over the side needs a permit to work, a risk assessment and the Chief Officer's approval. Before starting I make sure that:

- The **weather** and the ship's motion are suitable; it is not done in heavy weather or when the ship is underway at speed, unless the permit allows it.
- **Harnesses and lifelines** are inspected and attached to a strong point, and staging, ladders and bosun's chairs are in good condition and rigged correctly.
- **Tools** are secured so they cannot fall on people below, and the area below is closed off.
- When working over the side, a **lifebuoy with line** is ready and a person stays on deck to watch and communicate.
- The **bridge** knows about the work, and the radar, whistle and other equipment are isolated if people are working near them.

I check the work area myself before letting anyone start, and I stop the job if conditions change.

## Q: How do you manage deck stores and paint?

I keep an up-to-date inventory of deck stores: paint, ropes, wires, tools, PPE and spare parts. I check stock levels every month, and before stores are ordered I give the Chief Officer a list with the quantities based on the maintenance plan and the time until the next good supply port.

Paint and chemicals are stored in the paint locker, which must be well ventilated and have its fire-fighting arrangements working. I keep the safety data sheets available and make sure the crew uses the right PPE for each product. Paint is used on a first-in, first-out basis so it does not expire.

I also keep the stores tidy and secured for sea, because loose stores in bad weather are a danger and a waste. Careful store management helps the company control costs, and it means we never stop a job because something is missing.

## Q: A crew member keeps ignoring the safety rules. How do you deal with it?

First I stop the unsafe act immediately, because safety comes before any discussion. Then I talk to the person privately to understand why it happens: maybe he does not know the rule, does not have the right equipment, or is tired or under time pressure.

I explain the rule and the reason for it, and I show him how to do the job safely. If the problem was the equipment or the work plan, I fix that.

If it happens again, I give a clear warning and report it to the Chief Officer, so it is handled under the company's procedures. I am fair and consistent: the same rules apply to everyone, including my friends in the crew.

As a Bosun, I also lead by example. If I skip PPE or rush a job, the crew will do the same.
