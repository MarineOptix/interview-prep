## Q: How do you control fuel consumption and the ship's energy efficiency?

I start with accurate data: daily soundings, flow meter readings and consumption reports that match each other, so I can see trends and catch problems early.

The main areas I work on are:

- **Main engine condition**: correct fuel injection and combustion, clean turbochargers and scavenge air coolers, and regular performance measurements compared with the shop test.
- **Hull and propeller**: I report to the company when performance data shows fouling, so cleaning can be planned.
- **Auxiliary consumption**: running the right number of generators, avoiding unnecessary pumps and heating, and steam plant efficiency.
- **Speed and voyage optimisation** together with the Master, following charterer's and company instructions.

I follow the ship's SEEMP and the data collection for the IMO Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII), and I make sure the engine crew understands why efficiency matters, so they do not run equipment out of habit.

## Q: How do you prepare the engine department for a Port State Control inspection or class survey?

I keep the engine room ready at all times, but before an inspection I check the items inspectors look at most:

- **Emergency equipment**: emergency generator, emergency fire pump, quick-closing valves, emergency stops, fire dampers and fixed fire-fighting system, all tested and working.
- **Pollution prevention**: oily water separator, oil content meter and 15 ppm alarm working; Oil Record Book entries matching the soundings; no unofficial connections; MARPOL Annex VI documents, fuel samples and bunker delivery notes in order.
- **Machinery condition**: no leaks, good housekeeping, guards in place, bilges clean and dry.
- **Records**: PMS up to date, overdue jobs explained, certificates of class items valid.
- **Crew**: engineers able to demonstrate starting the emergency equipment and explain their duties.

I accompany the inspector myself, answer openly and never try to hide a defect. A known defect with a repair plan is acceptable; a hidden one destroys trust.

## Q: The ship has a blackout in a narrow channel. What do you do?

The priority is restoring power fast and safely, because the bridge has lost propulsion or steering.

1. **Confirm** the emergency generator has started and supplies the emergency switchboard: steering gear, emergency lighting, communications.
2. **Inform the bridge** immediately and keep the communication open, so the Master can anchor or use other measures.
3. **Restart** a main generator: if standby generators did not start automatically, start one manually, check it is safe to close the breaker and restore the main switchboard.
4. **Restart essential services in sequence**: lube oil, cooling water and fuel pumps, steering gear pumps, then prepare the main engine.
5. **Find the cause** before loading the plant again, for example a fuel supply problem, a dirty filter, a tripped breaker or an overload, to prevent a second blackout.

Afterwards I record the event, report it to the company and review the causes. Blackout drills help the engineers react quickly when it really happens.

## Q: How do you change over to compliant fuel before entering an Emission Control Area?

Under MARPOL Annex VI, ships in a sulphur ECA must use fuel with not more than 0.10% sulphur. The ship must carry a written fuel changeover procedure.

My steps:

1. **Plan the time** of changeover with the Master, based on the procedure and the system volumes, so that the changeover is fully completed before entering the ECA.
2. **Prepare the system**: check the compliant fuel tanks, the viscosity and temperature control, and the lubrication oil compatibility with the engine maker's recommendations.
3. **Change over gradually**, controlling the temperature change in the fuel system to avoid thermal shock and fuel pump sticking.
4. **Record** in the logbook the date, time, ship's position and the quantities of compliant fuel in each tank at the start and completion of the changeover, as MARPOL requires.

I also check the fuel's viscosity, because low-viscosity fuels can cause pump wear or problems starting the engine.

## Q: How do you manage and develop your engineers?

I see the Second Engineer as my right hand: he plans and runs the daily work and I support him with priorities, spare parts and decisions. I hold a short daily meeting to agree the plan, safety issues and any problem from the night.

I give each engineer clear responsibility for their equipment and expect them to know it well. I check the work, but I do not micromanage: people learn when they own a job.

For juniors and cadets I make sure they get proper familiarisation and real training, not only cleaning jobs. I involve them in overhauls and ask them to explain systems to me, which shows quickly what they understand.

I also look after the team: fair workloads, respect for rest hours and a working atmosphere where people report mistakes early. In my experience, an engine room where people are afraid to report problems is an unsafe engine room.
