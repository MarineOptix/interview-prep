## Q: What do you check when you take over the navigational watch?

I arrive early enough for my eyes to adjust at night, and I do not take over until I am satisfied. I check:

- **Position, course and speed** on the chart, and the ship's progress against the passage plan, including the next waypoint and wheel-over.
- **Traffic**: targets on radar and visually, and any close-quarters situations developing.
- **Weather and visibility**, and the forecast for my watch.
- **Navigational warnings** affecting the area.
- **Equipment**: steering mode, radars, ECDIS alarms, gyro and magnetic compass error, BNWAS, lights.
- **Master's standing and night orders**, and any special operations on deck or in the engine room.

If the officer I relieve is in the middle of a manoeuvre or avoiding action, the handover waits until it is complete. Finally I confirm "I have the watch" so there is no doubt about who is responsible.

## Q: What are the Third Officer's duties for life-saving and fire-fighting equipment?

On most ships the Third Officer is responsible for the maintenance of life-saving appliances, and often fire-fighting equipment, under the Chief Officer.

In practice I follow the SOLAS inspection routine and the planned maintenance system:

- **Weekly**: visual inspection of survival craft, rescue boats and launching appliances; lifeboat and rescue boat engines run, and the general alarm tested.
- **Monthly**: inspection of all life-saving appliances, including lifeboat equipment, using the checklist, with the results recorded in the logbook.
- **Regular checks** of lifebuoys, lifejackets, immersion suits, EPIRB and SART, pyrotechnics and their expiry dates, and portable fire extinguishers.
- **Servicing**: I make sure that items needing shore service, such as life rafts and hydrostatic releases, are serviced on time.

I keep the records ready for inspections and report any defect to the Chief Officer immediately, because an emergency is not the moment to discover that equipment does not work.

## Q: How do you know whether a risk of collision exists, and what do you do?

Under COLREG Rule 7, I use all available means. The most reliable sign is the compass bearing: if the bearing of an approaching vessel does not change appreciably, risk of collision exists. With large vessels or at close range, risk can exist even if the bearing is changing.

I confirm with radar plotting or ARPA, checking CPA and TCPA. I never make assumptions from scanty information, especially scanty radar information.

If I am the give-way vessel, for example in a crossing situation with the other ship on my starboard side under Rule 15, I take early and substantial action to keep well clear, usually a large alteration to starboard, so the other ship sees it clearly. I avoid crossing ahead of her.

If I am the stand-on vessel, I keep course and speed, but I am ready to act if the other ship does not take action, and I take action myself if a collision cannot be avoided by her action alone. In any doubt, I call the Master.

## Q: When do you call the Master?

I follow the Master's standing and night orders, and I call him early, never late. Typical situations are:

- Restricted visibility, or visibility expected to deteriorate.
- Traffic conditions or the movements of other vessels causing concern.
- Difficulty in maintaining course, or failure to sight land, a navigation mark or soundings when expected.
- Unexpected land, a navigation mark or a change in soundings.
- Breakdown of engines, steering or essential navigational equipment, or any alarm I cannot resolve.
- Heavy weather, or doubt about possible weather damage.
- Ship in a hazard to navigation, such as ice or a derelict.
- Any emergency, or any doubt at all.

Calling the Master does not mean giving him the watch. Until he says he has the conn, I remain responsible and keep taking the necessary action.

## Q: A crew member falls overboard during your watch. What do you do?

1. **Release a lifebuoy** with light and smoke signal from the bridge wing, as close to the person as possible.
2. **Press the MOB button** on the GPS or ECDIS to mark the position and time.
3. **Sound the alarm** (three long blasts, and the man overboard signal according to the company procedure) and call the Master.
4. **Start a recovery manoeuvre**: an Anderson turn if the person is still close, a Williamson turn if it is a delayed action or in poor visibility.
5. **Post lookouts** and assign one person to keep the person in sight and point at all times.
6. **Inform** the engine room and prepare the rescue boat; send an urgency or distress message to ships nearby and the coast station as appropriate.

After the rescue, the person needs medical care, especially for hypothermia, and the event is reported under the SMS.
