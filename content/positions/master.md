## Q: How do you understand the Master's overriding authority under the ISM Code?

The ISM Code requires the company to state clearly in the Safety Management System that the Master has the overriding authority and responsibility to make decisions on safety and pollution prevention, and to request the company's assistance when needed.

For me this means two things. First, if a commercial instruction from the office or the charterer conflicts with the safety of the crew, the ship or the environment, I will not follow it, and I will explain my decision in writing. Examples are sailing in weather the ship cannot handle, loading beyond stability limits or skipping rest hours to meet a schedule.

Second, authority comes with accountability. I use it rarely and I document it: the facts, the risk assessment, the decision and the communication with the company through the DPA. A Master who explains decisions early and clearly rarely has a conflict with the office.

## Q: How do you prepare your ship for a Port State Control inspection?

I treat PSC readiness as a daily standard, not a pre-arrival campaign, but before arrival I make sure the basics are checked:

- **Certificates and documents** valid and in one place, including crew certificates, medical certificates and seafarer employment agreements.
- **Records**: Oil Record Book, Garbage Record Book, rest hours, drills, ballast water records, and logbooks consistent with each other.
- **Life-saving and fire-fighting equipment**: lifeboats, fire doors, fire dampers, emergency fire pump and emergency generator in working order.
- **Crew familiarity**: inspectors often ask crew members to show their emergency duties or start the emergency fire pump. I run short drills so people are confident.
- **Open deficiencies** from earlier inspections closed with evidence.

During the inspection I accompany the inspector or assign a senior officer, answer honestly and never hide a defect. A defect that is reported, recorded and under repair is far better than one the inspector finds.

## Q: Your vessel runs aground. What are your immediate actions?

My priorities are people, then the environment, then the ship.

1. **Stop the engine** to avoid further damage, sound the general alarm and muster the crew; account for everyone.
2. **Assess damage**: sound all tanks and void spaces, check for water ingress and pollution, check the hull from deck and inspect the engine room.
3. **Display the correct lights and shapes**, and broadcast a warning to ships in the area; keep a proper watch.
4. **Calculate stability and stresses** with the new conditions, using the loading computer and the company's emergency response service if available.
5. **Inform the company** through the emergency contact and the DPA, the coastal state, and follow the SOPEP if there is any oil leak.
6. **Record everything** and preserve the evidence, including the VDR data.

I would not try to refloat the ship until I understand the damage and stability, and agreed the plan with the company and the authorities. An early, uncontrolled refloat can sink a damaged ship.

## Q: A crew member reports for duty under the influence of alcohol. What do you do?

Safety first: the person is relieved from duty immediately and is not allowed to keep a watch or do any work. STCW sets a limit of 0.05% blood alcohol content, or 0.25 mg/l in breath, for watchkeeping duties, and most company policies are stricter, often zero tolerance.

Then I follow the company's drug and alcohol policy step by step: a test with witnesses if the policy requires it, a written statement from the person and the witnesses, an entry in the official log, and a report to the company. I also check whether alcohol is coming on board in a way that breaks the policy.

I handle it fairly and privately. The person is entitled to explain and to see the record. At the same time, I make sure the rest of the crew knows the rule is applied to everyone, regardless of rank or nationality. Consistency is what makes a policy work.

## Q: When would you take over the conn from a pilot?

The pilot is an adviser, and the Master remains responsible for the safe navigation of the ship. I start with a proper Master–pilot information exchange: the passage plan, the ship's manoeuvring characteristics, the equipment status and the tug and mooring plan. The bridge team monitors the pilot's actions against that plan.

I would challenge the pilot first, clearly and early, if I see the ship deviating from the agreed plan, a dangerous speed, a misunderstood order or a developing close-quarters situation. If the answer does not resolve the doubt, or there is no time, I take over the conn, inform the pilot and VTS, and act to make the ship safe.

Examples would be a pilot who appears unwell or under the influence, or whose orders would put the ship into danger. After the event I record the facts and report to the company and, where required, to the port authority.
