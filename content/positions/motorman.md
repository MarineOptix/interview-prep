## Q: What are your daily duties as a Motorman?

I work under the engineers, usually the Second Engineer, and my daily duties are:

- **Rounds** of the engine room with the engineer on watch: checking levels, temperatures, pressures and leaks.
- **Lubrication** of machinery according to the lubrication chart.
- **Cleaning**: purifiers, filters and strainers, and keeping the engine room clean and the bilges dry.
- **Soundings** of tanks and reporting them to the engineer.
- **Assisting** the engineers during maintenance and overhauls.
- **Stores**: helping to receive and stow spare parts and lube oil.

During bunkering and in emergencies I follow my duties on the muster list. I always tell the engineer when I finish a job or find a problem.

## Q: How do you clean a purifier safely?

I clean the purifier only after the engineer has stopped it and given permission.

1. The purifier is **stopped and isolated**: feed and water valves closed, the motor isolated and tagged out at the starter.
2. I wait until the bowl has **stopped completely**, using the brake if fitted. A purifier bowl spins at very high speed and keeps turning for a long time after it is stopped.
3. I dismantle the bowl with the **special tools**, following the manual, and clean the discs and parts. Discs are handled carefully because they are sharp and can be damaged.
4. I check the **seals and O-rings** and tell the engineer if they need changing.
5. I assemble the bowl in the **correct order** with the right markings aligned.

I wear gloves and goggles because of hot oil and chemicals. The engineer checks the assembly before the purifier is started again.

## Q: How do you take tank soundings and report them?

I take soundings with the sounding tape or read the remote level gauges, depending on the tank, as the engineer instructs.

With a sounding tape, I lower it slowly until it touches the striking plate at the bottom, then read the level of the liquid on the tape. For tanks that may have water, I use water-finding paste. I always close the sounding pipe cap after sounding, because an open sounding pipe can cause pollution or flooding.

I write down the tank name, the sounding and the time, and give it to the engineer, who calculates the volume from the tank tables. If a level has changed unexpectedly compared with the last sounding, I report it immediately, because it can mean a leak or a valve left open.

## Q: You find a lube oil leak from a pipe flange on the main engine. What do you do?

1. **Report it to the engineer on watch** immediately, with the exact location and how fast it is leaking.
2. **Check whether the oil is reaching a hot surface**, such as the exhaust pipe or turbocharger. If yes, it is a fire risk and I warn the engineer that it is urgent.
3. **Contain the leak** with a drip tray or absorbent material if it is safe, without touching hot parts or moving parts.
4. **Help the engineer** stop and repair the leak as instructed.
5. **Clean up** the oil after the repair and put the rags into the oily waste container.

I do not try to tighten the flange of a running system myself unless the engineer tells me to, because it can make the leak worse.

## Q: What precautions do you take when lifting heavy parts in the engine room?

Before lifting, I check with the engineer that the lifting plan is clear: the weight of the part, the lifting points and who gives the signals.

- **Equipment**: the chain block, slings and shackles must have a safe working load larger than the weight, be inspected and certified, and be in good condition.
- **Lifting points**: only approved lifting eyes or beams, never pipes or rails.
- **People**: no one stands under the load, and one person gives the signals.
- **Movement**: lift slowly, use guide ropes to control the load, and pay attention to the ship's motion at sea.
- **Landing**: put the load down on wood or a safe surface and secure it so it cannot move.

I wear a helmet, gloves and safety shoes, and I stop the lift if something looks wrong.
